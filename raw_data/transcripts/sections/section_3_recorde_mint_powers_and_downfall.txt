# Part 3: 조폐국 임명, 레코드의 독자적 기하학적 표기법, 파멸과 등호(=)의 탄생 (11:54 - 17:40)

For Robert Record, that real-world context was most directly connected to money. Because as important as his books would be for English math students, Record wasn't making a living from them. But soon a new opportunity would present itself. Recently, King Henry VIII had been raising money for the crown by reducing the amount of precious metal inside of English coins, debasing the currency, as it's called. But during this great debasement, it was easy for someone in charge of a mint to skim a little off the top for themselves. The corruption only got worse after Henry VIII died and his 9-year-old son, Edward VI, replaced him. One of these scandals forced out the head of the Bristol mint. King Edward's government needed to clean things up, so they had to find a replacement with a reputation for being meticulous and honest. Robert Record was their man. He was given the job at four times his predecessor's salary, but it only took a week in his new role before he attracted an enemy. In The Whetstone of Witte, Record wanted his students to know about the German method of surds, solids, and embedded powers, but he also wanted to simplify things to make the ideas more visual and easier to understand. So, Record also proposes an alternative to the German approach. He starts with his unit, and And he gives a line of units to represent his root number or what we would call X. Then to represent his square, he makes an actual square. And for cubics, he gives an actual cube. And then he devises a creative way to represent the higher dimensions visually, too. If you take the cube that you just formed and make it into your new unit, then you can basically start over with the progression. Multiplying the cube by the base number one more time gives a single file line of cubes, which Record calls a long cube. Multiplying again gives a square of cubes, and then one more multiplication gives a cube of cubes. You could make the cube of cubes your new unit and just keep going with this pattern forever without ever needing to invent any new names, because when you multiply like this, you just keep going through the cycle. We can see that even though his names were new, the idea of using multiplication to derive higher degree unknowns was actually as old as Diophantus. Although, Robert Record did land on one innovation. In order to avoid tediously writing the phrase is equal to over and over again, like most other books did, Record decided to just write two horizontal parallel lines. He said he chose those because no two things can be more equal. Robert Record couldn't possibly have known it, but those two lines would eventually become his most lasting legacy. William Herbert, the Earl of Pembroke, had his eye on Record. Herbert was an illiterate nobleman who had a reputation as a mad fighting fellow. 7 days after Record's appointment as Comptroller of the Bristol Mint, Herbert tried to intercept valuables bound for the king coming from an iron mill that Record also managed. When Record refused to hand them over, Herbert accused Record of treason. The case went to trial and it seems to have ended with minor penalties for Record. But, that was just the beginning of Robert Record's dealings with William Herbert. By late 1553, Edward the VI had died at the age of 15 from a lung infection and soon Mary the I emerged as queen. Queen Mary immediately moved to crack down on the corruption at the mints. Believing the political winds were finally blowing his favor, Robert Record sent Mary a letter detailing Herbert's interference over the course of the past several years across both the Bristol mint and the Dublin mint, which by now Record was also supervising. But, in the years since their first confrontation, Herbert's political power had grown. In response to the letter, Herbert sued Record for libel demanding 12,000 pounds in damages. That's like a few million dollars today. And even though most of Record's accusations were probably accurate, Herbert brought influential witnesses to his side and he ultimately won a judgment of 1,000 pounds against Record. That was a lot of money that Record probably didn't have. The government had been slow to pay his salary from the mint and when he didn't pay the fine, he was sent to debtors' prison. By the time the Whetstone of Wit was published in 1557, Record was probably already at King's Bench prison in London. 1557 and 1558 were pandemic years, probably influenza, and by mid-1558, Robert Record was dead. The man whose arithmetic book would teach English math students for the next 150 years didn't even get a proper gravestone. 12 years later, Record's heirs received a final payment for his services rendered to the Irish mint in the amount of 1,000 pounds. Meanwhile, the Whetstone of Witte didn't have the success that Recorde's arithmetic book, The Grounde of Artes, had had. The Grounde of Artes would be reprinted over 49 times and become the basic arithmetic textbook for English math students. The Whetstone, on the other hand, with Recorde's algebra and his naming of the powers and his equal sign, only ever got a single printing.
