One person who read Viète was a man who'd grown up in ill health, known for lying in bed thinking until 11:00 every morning. He'd written that algebra had become a confused and obscure art. And he had aspirations to find a wholly new science by which it will be possible to solve all questions that can be proposed with any kinds of quantities. This was not a humble man. But after René Descartes absorbed the math of his predecessors, he unified geometry and algebra into a single system. And in the process, he came up with his own notation. X and the other letters from the end of the alphabet would be unknown values, while letters from the beginning of the alphabet like A, B, and C would represent undetermined coefficients. Instead of circles or arcs, Descartes just made the index numbers of his powers slightly elevated after the variable. X cubed becomes X superscript 3. Now, for X squared, Descartes often just stuck with XX since it takes up the same amount of space on the page anyway. But from here, the template was set. Descartes didn't know about Recorde's equal sign though, so he used this little swoop which might have started as a shorthand for the Latin word equalis. Now, Descartes was also writing about philosophy and science, and he was more interested in reaching a general audience than the academic elite who he scorned. So, he wrote his books in French. But at the same time, Descartes didn't seem to care much about making his math easy to understand. Throughout La Géométrie, he skips over details in his examples, and he leaves the reader to figure out specifics for themselves. Between writing in the wrong language for scholars and being lazy about clear communication, Descartes' geometry book could have easily been bound for obscurity. That is, until a Dutch associate named Frans van Schooten recognized the importance of Descartes' work. Descartes published his book in 1637 and then walked away from mathematics entirely. But in the decades to come, van Schooten would release annotated Latin translations, finally making La Géométrie available to scholars across Europe. Now, across these centuries, there had been a gradual shift in how people engage with books. As a math book changed from being something that you're meant to memorize and recite out loud to something that's meant to be read, the notation itself no longer had to translate directly back into spoken language. And gradually, this meant that the syntax of the notation could start to lead the semantics. In other words, once you're not anchored to a verbal explanation of what you're doing, you can follow the rules of the notation wherever they lead, even if the result pulls away from the ideas' original semantic meaning. It's in this context that our familiar explanations of fractional and negative exponents start to make sense. Once we know that multiplying two things with the same base adds the exponents, and that dividing subtracts, then the notation automatically tells us what a negative exponent should mean. 2 ^ 3 / 2 ^ 5 = 1/4, but by following the division rule of subtracting the exponents, we can see that this also equals 2 ^ -2. Similarly, if we know that raising a power to a power means that you multiply the powers, then applying this fact to the exponent notation gives us a meaning for fractional exponents. We know that squaring a square root brings us back to the original value, but since powers of powers also multiply, and 2 * 1/2 = 1, a square root must correspond to the exponent of 1/2. By following the notation where it led us, what had once been separate concepts handled in a variety of different ways can now all come together under one roof. In 1655, while working out some of the foundational ideas that would eventually lead to integral calculus, finding the area under a curve, John Wallis used Descartes' notation for positive integer exponents, even though he later claimed that Descartes stole his ideas from fellow Englishman Thomas Harriot. But, Wallis then suggests informally that exponents could potentially be fractional or even irrational, similar to Simon Stevin's earlier work. Wallis doesn't put it into practice right away, but the first written example of full-fledged use of modern fractional exponents came in a letter written on June 13th, 1676 by another Englishman who had spent time working at the Mint, Isaac Newton. Newton had come up with a generalized binomial theorem, which works for any rational exponent and sets aside the idea of exponentiation as being simply repeated multiplication. In the generations after Newton, following the syntax of exponents even further allowed mathematicians like Johann Bernoulli and later Leonhard Euler to describe an idea of exponential functions, where the exponent itself can be any continuous value. Euler eventually changed the meaning of exponents even further by bringing in imaginary and complex values for exponents. But, there's another interesting feature here. See, when Newton was a college student, he was unsatisfied with the math being taught at Cambridge. So, he used Van Schooten's translation of Descartes and an algebra book written by one of his countrymen, William Oughtred, to teach himself the latest techniques. Oughtred's algebra book had eclipsed Robert Recorde's Whetstone of Witte as the standard text for advanced students. But, Oughtred's book would be the key link in the chain that taught Robert Recorde's equal sign to a new generation of British mathematicians.
