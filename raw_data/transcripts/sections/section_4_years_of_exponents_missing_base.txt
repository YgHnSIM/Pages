# Part 4: 지수 기호의 진화와 밑(Base)의 결여 문제 (17:40 - 20:48)

Back on the mainland, for years there had been others who tried out different ways to write the powers of the unknowns. In France, 100 years before Recorde, Nicolas Chuquet created a notation that named his species by writing the index numbers as a superscript. He even showed constant numbers to have an index of zero. But, this manuscript didn't see the light of day until the 1800s. The only person that we know interacted with it at the time was Étienne de la Roche. De la Roche used some of Chuquet's work in his own writings, but he didn't think the superscript notation was important, so he didn't use it. Around the same time that Recorde was writing his books, a German monk named Michael Stifel wrote Arithmetica Integra, which includes this table of the powers of two, showing a list of index numbers above it. Stifel called these numbers the exposed numbers, thus coining the term exponent. A couple decades after Recorde's death, Rafael Bombelli took another approach. He also named his powers using the indices, but he put them inside of little arcs, like this. Soon after, Simon Stevin made his own variation by just putting circles around the index numbers. Stevin even suggested that the circles could go around fractions in the same way to indicate square roots, and that a fraction like 2/3 would represent two separate operations, involution and evolution. In other words, raising to a power and then separately taking a root. But maybe you've noticed that something's missing here. The problem wasn't actually with writing the index numbers. They had those. What they didn't have was a base. Cossic algebra and abacus algebra only really accommodated a single unknown. So there was no need to give it any kind of special name. It's just always assumed to be there. When you see the two with a circle around it in Simon Stevin's book, that character just stands for the squared unknown. A three followed by two with a circle basically means 3x squared. But for a modern idea of exponents to be possible, one that just works for any kind of constant, variable, or any other expression, the notation needed to indicate the base. And that breakthrough came unintentionally in 1591 when François Viète was looking for a way to use algebra for geometry. Geometric constructions automatically have many different lengths, some known and some unknown. So Viète started using letters to represent all of these quantities. In finding a solution to his narrow and specific problem, he accidentally revolutionized algebra. Soon everywhere you looked, people were using letters to represent known and unknown values. But even so, nobody could agree on how to write the powers of these values.
