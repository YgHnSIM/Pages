# Part 2: 이슬람 대수학과 전근대적 수 개념 (07:10 - 11:54)

In the time before Robert Record, generations of mathematicians had already spent centuries studying the powers of the unknown. For most of this time, people could only solve quadratic equations, but these higher powers were a source of constant interest. Arabic mathematicians named their unknowns in a similar way to Diophantus, although most of the time they wrote everything out in words, just like al-Khwarizmi, the author of the first Arabic algebra book. Although he never went above quadratics. In 11th century Baghdad, al-Karaji also had tables that showed how to do operations with reciprocal powers. These are what we would describe as negative exponents, but al-Karaji handled them like fractions. Now, his work didn't make it to Europe, but algebra did, and soon an Italian tradition known as abaco algebra flourished. Many cities and towns had a resident abaco master. This was a person who usually made a living teaching basic arithmetic to local children, but then they would demonstrate their mastery of algebra by competing to solve problems against other abaco practitioners. Of course, nobody could have known what the future would bring mathematically. So, during this time, we find an ocean of false starts, of ideas that popped up somewhere, but then didn't amount to anything, and even outright mistakes. In Paris, Nicola Oresme proposed the idea of fractional proportions, which basically worked the same as our fractional exponents, but Oresme's work didn't spread very far, and it didn't seem to influence others. Eventually, some Italian mathematicians started to make the names of the powers easier to write using abbreviations. When abacus algebra found its way to Germany and became Cossic algebra, German mathematicians started using little glyphs to represent their unknown numbers. But, despite the new notation, the system itself hadn't changed much. And by the early 1500s, most people were still using these crazy names like the zenza zenza zenzic. There had been progress, but as far as a system for naming the powers, things didn't look all that much different from what Diophantus and the Arabic mathematicians had been doing. It's tempting for us to look at this and wonder why nobody seemed to see what we would consider to be the obvious answer. Why don't you just write the index number for the power to show the number of repeated multiplications the way we do with exponents? But these were the greatest mathematicians of their day, and they had deeper reasons to do things the way they did. One of them has to do with numbers themselves. Before the 17th century, numbers were thought of differently from the way we think of numbers today. At the time, a number was an adjective describing the quantity of some specific type of thing. Numbers came in what's called quantity species pairs. It's like how the number dozen doesn't really exist by itself. Unlike the number three, the word dozen is only meaningful if it's used to describe how many eggs or bagels or whatever you have. It's describing a tangible thing rather than an abstract concept. So while today the expression 3x tells us to multiply an abstract number three by some other abstract unknown number x, the pre-modern version of this, three things, means that you're taking three of some unknown thing. There's no multiplication involved, and you can think of those unknown things as being like three coins of unknown denomination. I know this distinction sounds subtle and maybe even a little pointless, but it actually had a huge impact on how people did math. Because back then, each power of the unknown was seen as a different type or a different species. So saying two squares, five things, and four numbers is kind of like saying 3 hours, 21 minutes, and 13 seconds. Each part has a different unit to it, which can't be combined without doing some kind of conversion. And this changes how you would approach a basic operation like multiplication. Multiplying different powers together involves not just multiplying the quantities, but also changing the species accordingly. So you would have a list that shows the relationship between the types, kind of like the way we might map a relationship between your father and your great-great-grandfather. The name describes the relationships, but it's not set up to do a mathematical operation directly. There's like this extra step in between. And while we might think this is all awkward and inconvenient, in the context of the time, it made sense. Since it also meant that numbers were fundamentally grounded in a real-world context.
