# Part 1: 고대 기원과 코식(Cossic) 대수학의 등장 (00:00 - 07:10)

The word with the most Z's in the Oxford English Dictionary is zenzizenzizenzic. It comes from a 16th century math book, which says that it doth represent the square of squares squarely. So, something raised to the eighth power. Zenzizenzizenzic was part of the German system to describe what we now call exponents. But the author of this English book, Robert Record, wanted to teach it better. He brought arithmetic and algebra into the English language for the first time in history, inventing the equal sign in the process. But yet, in this final book before he died in prison, Robert Record couldn't crack exponents. But this story is much bigger than anyone person. So, why did it take 1,900 years to invent exponents? The story of exponents starts in the 3rd century BCE with somebody doing about what you might expect, trying to write extremely large numbers. Archimedes wanted to estimate the number of grains of sand that could fit in the universe, but since ancient Greek numbers couldn't count past 100 million, he needed to invent his own system for counting even larger numbers. In the process of doing this, he wrote out the first basic rule for exponents. In modern terms, this is the fact that multiplying two values with the same base adds their powers. But since Archimedes didn't have the notation to describe repeated multiplication the way we do with exponents, he put it all in the context of geometric sequences or what was called continued proportions. About 500 years later in the 3rd century CE, the mathematician Diophantus introduced notation for unknowns and powers of unknowns, which he used to solve number theory problems. Diophantus' work would be lost and found again several times in the centuries to come, but this focus on the powers of unknowns would be where the action was as far as the history of exponents goes. In the mid-1500s, Robert Record had studied European algebra and he wanted to bring it into English. When he was a kid growing up as the son of a merchant in Tenby, Wales, there were no English language math books. If you wanted to learn math, you needed to be able to read Latin. Record's arithmetic book, The Ground of Arts, was among the first to change that. And his final book, The Whetstone of Wit, would be the first ever algebra book written in English. Now, Robert Record didn't know the full origins of the math that he was teaching here, but zenza zenzic or square of squared square was his Anglicized version of the German term zenzizenzizenzem or zensus zensui de zenzero. He would have learned it from the German algebra or cos books that were current on the mainland at the time. Zenso comes from the Italian word censo, which itself is based on the Latin census, meaning something like a property assessment. Census was the translation of the Arabic word mal or wealth, but which was used in Arabic algebra to refer to a squared unknown value, basically what we call x squared. Record writes that the idea behind a zenza zenzic number is that you can take the square root of it three times, like you can with the number 256. Square of that, 16, is a zenza zenzic number, so you can take the square root again to get four, which itself is a perfect square or a zenzic, giving a final root of two. In our terminology, taking these repeated roots multiplies their powers. So, 2 * 2 * 2 is equivalent to an eighth power. Using the same approach, a zenza cubic, a square of cubes, is equivalent to a sixth power, and a cubic cube is the same as a ninth power. So, in Record's telling, this system describes powers based on how to find their bases by taking repeated square and cube roots. Since people had algorithms to find those two types of roots by hand, this naming makes perfect sense. But, not every power is a multiple of only two and three, so you also need other names for the other prime powers. Record's English version of what he calls the vulgar or common name for a fifth power is a sursolid, and a seventh power is a second sursolid. This Cossic algebra was considered to be a very advanced topic, by the way. It was only taught to people who wanted to learn math for its own sake, because it didn't have a practical purpose. It's sort of like math Olympiad problems or something, where you do it cuz you want to sharpen your mind and learn to be a better problem solver. In fact, that's where Record's title, The Whetstone of Wit, comes from. It's a pun. See, Coss is also the German word for whetstone, as in the thing you use to sharpen a blade. So, the book is both an algebra book and a tool to sharpen your wit. But, for someone who was drawn to math for its own sake, Robert Record's life was very much a part of the tumult of his time. He found his way to bigger things thanks to his education, attending Oxford and then Cambridge before moving to London. As both a mathematician and a physician, Record developed a reputation for meticulous attention to accuracy and detail. Those were the qualities that would fuel his rise within London society, but they would also eventually lead to his catastrophic downfall. And he couldn't have known it, but he was also living near the end of an era for mathematics. In just a few decades, algebra, as it had been done for hundreds of years would change dramatically. And in the process, the notation for exponents would be stripped down to its barest essentials. The complicated names would be replaced by a simple, easy-to-understand notation. And once those unnecessary parts were gone, the design that remained would be one that endured for the ages.
