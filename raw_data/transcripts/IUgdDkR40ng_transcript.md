Drop a pin on a map and your phone gives that spot two numbers, its latitude and longitude. An identity card no other place on Earth shares. Giving every point of a curve a card like that feels obvious, yet the greatest geometers in history never once did it. And for almost 2,000 years, one problem stayed unsolved because of it. Give every point of a circle its card and the whole circle collapses into one short line, x² + y² = 1. Because every point on the curve fits that line and every point off it fails, which means the equation is the circle itself written in another language. For most of history though, those were two separate languages with no way to translate between them. Since geometry could see shapes but couldn't calculate with them, while algebra could calculate but couldn't see anything at all. So the question is simple enough, who built that translation? Yet the answer is one of the strangest stories in mathematics because it runs through a problem about four lines and a moving point that beat the greatest Greek geometers and then stayed unsolved for 1,300 years. A former soldier who did his best thinking lying in bed and a quiet lawyer who wrote it all down a year before him and never had it printed. In the end, the system every student learns ended up carrying the name of only one of them. And the reason begins with the people who came closest and still fell short. Around 200 BCE, a Greek mathematician named Apollonius of Perga wrote eight books on the curves you get when you slice a cone with a flat plane, and tilting the plane changes what you get from an ellipse to a parabola to a hyperbola. He described each curve so precisely that if you translate his sentences into modern symbols, his description of the parabola becomes y squared equals p times x, the very equation students write today. Still, he wrote it as a long sentence about squares and rectangles rather than as an equation, and more importantly, he could only describe a curve that already existed because he had built it by slicing a cone. He could never start from a relationship between two numbers and ask what curve that relationship would draw. So, the points of his curves never got cards of their own. That weakness stayed hidden until around 320 CE. When Pappus of Alexandria wrote down a problem the ancient geometers had never managed to finish. Imagine four straight lines fixed on a page, and a single point free to move around them, where the point has to obey one rule. Its distances to the first two lines multiplied together must always stay in the same proportion to its distances to the other two lines multiplied together. Since the point can only move in ways that obey the rule, it traces out a path, and the question is what that path is. Pappus admitted that even Apollonius hadn't fully solved it, and when the problem grew to five, six, or more lines, he wrote that nobody had worked out a single one of those curves, not even the simplest. For the next 1,300 years, it sat in his book as an open challenge. Until one winter, it landed on the desk of a man who had spent years waiting for exactly this kind of test. Late in 1631, a Leiden professor named Golius handed Pappus's problem to a 35-year-old Frenchman named Descartes. A former soldier who did his best thinking lying in bed. 12 years earlier, shut away in a stove-heated room in Germany, he had become convinced that geometry and algebra could be joined into a single method. And Pappus's problem was the perfect way to prove it. Because if his method could crack something that had beaten Euclid and Apollonius, nobody could ever dismiss it. So, try solving it the way anyone in 1631 would have, starting with geometry. With four lines, you multiply two distances and get an area. So, you're comparing one rectangle with another. Which the Greeks could at least picture. With six lines, you multiply three distances and get a volume. Which is harder, but still something you can hold in your mind. With eight lines, though, you'd have to multiply four lengths together. And that would need a fourth direction that space simply doesn't have. Which is why Pappus wrote that nothing can be contained by more than three dimensions. So, geometry fails because the problem asks for a shape that can't exist. What about algebra, then? In 1591, a French lawyer named Viète had started writing quantities as letters, which meant you could finally write a whole family of equations at once. Yet, Viète kept the Greek rule as the first law of his algebra, insisting that every term in an equation must have the same dimension, so you could never add a length to an area. And a fourth power stood for a magnitude of a fourth kind that nobody could draw. Algebra fails too, then, and for the same reason, because it had inherited the Greek belief that multiplying lengths always builds bigger and bigger shapes. This is where Descartes did something nobody had dared to do, because instead of solving the problem inside the old rules, he changed what multiplication means. Pick any length you like and call it one. Now, draw two lines meeting at a corner. Mark the unit and a length of two along the first line, mark a length of three along the second, and join the end of the unit to the end of the three. If you draw a parallel line through the end of the two, it meets the second line at a distance of exactly six, so 2 × 3 has come out as a length instead of a rectangle. With that one construction, the wall comes down. Since x squared is now just a line, and so are x cubed and x to the fourth, while the fourth dimension Pappus called meaningless isn't needed at all. Descartes wrote that when he said square or cube, he meant only simple lines, and he kept the old words only because they were familiar from algebra. With multiplication freed, Pappus's problem fell quickly. Descartes picked one of the given lines as his baseline and gave the moving point the identity card the Greeks had never given it, just two numbers, x for how far along the baseline you go, and y for how far you rise from it. Because every line and every angle is fixed, similar triangles turn each distance into something very plain, a number times x plus a number times y plus a constant. Take four lines on a page, say the bottom edge, the left edge, a line one unit up, and a slanted line crossing both edges at two. And if you measure the slanted one straight across, the four distances become y, x, 1 - y, and 2 - x - y. Taking the simplest ratio, one, the rule says y * x must equal (1 - y)(2 - x - y). Expanding the right side looks as if it should make things worse, yet an xy term appears on both sides and cancels, which leaves x = (y - 1)(y - 2). That's a parabola, and the moving point traces exactly that curve. The same thing happens for any four lines, because multiplying two first-degree expressions can never give anything higher than the second degree, and every second degree equation draws a conic. With more lines, the degree simply goes up, so the curves the Greeks couldn't name became families sorted by the degree of their equations. Within five or six weeks, Descartes had solved what Euclid and Apollonius couldn't, and by the spring of 1632, he wrote to a friend that he had done it, unaware that someone in France was already working toward the same idea. Still, the real achievement was bigger than one problem, because he had shown that a curve and its equation are the same object, which meant you could now start from an equation and let it draw its own curve. But was Descartes really the first? Because at the same time, in the city of Toulouse, in the south of France, someone else was crossing the same bridge from the other side. Fermat was a lawyer who did his mathematics privately after his days in court. Around 1629, he set out to rebuild one of Apollonius' books that had been lost, and while working with Viète's letters, he noticed that when an equation has two unknowns, they can't change independently, since whenever one moves, the other is forced to move with it. He drew one baseline, measured the first unknown along it, raised the second unknown from that spot, and watched the tip of the second length trace out a path, so every point on that path carried its own pair of numbers. He wrote that whenever two unknowns appear in a final equation, the end of one of them traces a straight line or a curve, and he showed that first-degree equations always draw straight lines, while second-degree equations draw circles and conics. Fermat had all of this written down by 1636, which was a year before Descartes published anything. Yet he never printed it, so it passed around only in handwritten copies until his son published it in 1679. Fermat had still kept Viète's rule of dimensions, yet the translation itself had been found twice by two men coming from opposite directions, since Descartes went from a geometry problem to an equation, while Fermat went from an equation to a curve, and neither of them knew what the other was doing. Descartes published first in 1637 in a book called La Géométrie. And it's there that he chose letters from the start of the alphabet, A, B, and C for known values, and letters from the end, X, Y, and Z for unknowns, which is the real reason X became the unknown in every classroom. You may have heard that X comes from an Arabic word for thing that Spanish translators couldn't spell, yet there's no evidence for that story. Once the two men learned about each other, though, the discovery they shared turned into a war. When Fermat read Descartes's essay on light, he wrote that the reasoning didn't hold together. And Descartes, who wasn't used to being corrected by a provincial lawyer, replied that Fermat had only looked at his work cross-eyed. Fermat responded by sending his method for drawing tangent lines to curves, and in January 1638, Descartes declared that it contained an obvious logical error. Then he set Fermat a test, a curve where x and y are tangled together, x³ + y³ = 3xy, which forms a loop with two long tails. Fermat's method is easiest to see on a simple curve like y = x². You nudge x forward by a tiny amount he called e. Compare (x + e) all squared with x squared, and the difference is 2xe + e². When you divide by e, you get 2x + e, and when you finally let e drop away, you're left with 2x, the slope of the tangent at that point. That 2x is exactly the slope Newton would reach a generation later with his tiny quantity o. Descartes had his own tangent method, which was exact but painfully slow, while Fermat's method went straight through the tangled curve and gave the tangent at any point. And at the tip of the loop, it comes out to a slope of exactly minus one. Descartes had dismissed the method as a lucky guess its author didn't understand. Yet, as Fermat's explanations kept arriving, he conceded that the method worked for tangents. By October 1638, the man who had called Fermat's work an obvious error wrote to him directly saying he had never known anyone who showed that they knew more geometry than Fermat did. So, Descartes lost the battle and still he won the war, which is why we say Cartesian coordinates and not Fermatian ones. His book was printed while Fermat's waited 42 years for a printer. And his notation was simply easier to use. There's one more surprise here because the grid we draw today with two perpendicular axes crossing at zero and negative numbers spreading left and down isn't in Descartes's book at all. He used a single baseline, measured his second length at whatever angle the problem needed, and treated negative answers with suspicion, calling them false roots. The rest was built by those who came after him over the next 100 years. The most important reader of that book, though, was a young student at Cambridge. Around 1664, Newton picked up the book, and he later recalled that he read a few pages, got stuck, went back to the beginning, read a little further, and kept starting over until he had mastered it. Once curves had become equations, a new question was waiting on every page. Because if an equation tells you exactly where a point is, it should also tell you how steeply the curve is rising at that exact point. Fermat's tiny e had already answered it for particular curves. And Newton, building on both men, turned it into a general method. In the end, Descartes's name went on to every graph. His x became the unknown in every classroom. And Fermat's tiny e became the first step toward calculus. Today, every pin you drop on a map, every pixel on your screen, and every point a computer calculates with carries that same kind of card. So, the next time you draw a circle and write x² + y² = 1 beside it, you're using an idea that took nearly 2,000 years to find, and that two rivals discovered at almost the same moment.
